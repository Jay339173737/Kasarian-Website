MCO 1 — Gender Roles Website

Gender roles in the Philippines, from the babaylan to now

This site looks at how ideas about gender in the Philippines have changed over time — shaped first by old traditions, then by 300 years of colonization, and still being debated today in government, at home, and on TV. It's been 500 years, and people are still arguing about it.

Five turning points

History

How gender roles here were shaped, broken down, and rebuilt — long before "gender roles" was even a phrase people used. Each time period left something behind that still affects how people think today.

Before 1565 — Pre-colonial societies

The babaylan

Long before the Spanish arrived, many Filipino communities had a respected leader called the babaylan (also called katalonan, asog, or bayoguin, depending on the region). They were healers and spiritual leaders, and they held the community's knowledge about farming, medicine, and science — historian Zeus Salazar even called them the "proto-scientists" of early Filipino society. The babaylan worked closely with the datu, the community's political leader, and could take charge of the whole community if the datu wasn't around.

Most babaylan were women, but some were men who dressed and lived as women, according to early Spanish records. Back then, gender was based more on what job someone did, how they dressed, and how they acted — not just on their body. That's why many experts today point to the babaylan as proof that Filipino society didn't always split people strictly into "man" and "woman."

1565–1898 — Spanish colonial period

A new "ideal woman"

Spanish priests saw the babaylan as a threat to their Catholic religion. They called the babaylan's practices "witchcraft" and worked to wipe out the role over 300 years of colonization. Male babaylan were targeted the hardest, since they didn't fit the strict male-or-female categories the priests wanted to enforce.

In place of the babaylan came a new idea of the "ideal woman" — quiet, modest, and skilled in housework like embroidery. Priests enforced this through confession, where they also controlled women's sex lives directly. This is actually where most "traditional" Filipino gender rules come from — even though people often assume they're much older, or somehow more naturally Filipino.

1898–1946 — American colonial period

School, then the vote

Under American rule, public schools opened to girls as well as boys. This let a whole generation of Filipino women become teachers, office workers, and eventually activists. That activism built up to a big vote in 1937 — but there was a catch: the 1935 Constitution said women could only win the right to vote if at least 300,000 women voted "yes."

Women worked hard to hit that number. On April 30, 1937, 447,725 women voted yes — nearly 150,000 more than needed — against only 44,307 who voted no. Filipino women didn't just win the right to vote by a small margin. They won it by showing up in huge numbers.

1946–1986 — Postwar Philippines and Martial Law

Fighting the dictatorship

Women's activism quieted down for a while after World War II, then picked back up hard during the Marcos dictatorship. Women were among those arrested, tortured, and made to disappear during Martial Law. Some who survived that period went on to build the modern women's movement almost directly out of that resistance.

In 1984, around 10,000 women marched in Manila against the Marcos government. That protest led to the creation of GABRIELA — short for General Assembly Binding Women for Reforms, Integrity, Equality, Leadership, and Action — named after Gabriela Silang, an 18th-century Filipina who led an armed revolt against Spanish rule after her husband was killed. GABRIELA is still one of the biggest women's groups in the country today.

1986–present — From EDSA to ongoing debate

Slow progress

When the Marcos dictatorship fell in 1986, it opened the door for years of activism to finally turn into real laws. That eventually led to the Responsible Parenthood and Reproductive Health Act of 2012 — a law that took over a decade to pass, and only happened once the president backed it.

The same thing is playing out right now with the SOGIE Equality Bill, covered in the next section: strong public support, years of advocacy, and a bill that still hasn't reached the president's desk. History here doesn't move forward smoothly — it moves when enough people refuse to give up.

The numbers underneath the ranking

Where things stand today

People often say the Philippines is a regional leader on gender equality — and in some ways, that's true. But if you look past the headline number, the real picture is more mixed.

20th

Global Gender Gap ranking

Out of 148 countries in the World Economic Forum's 2025 report. The Philippines scored 78.1% on gender equality — the highest score in Asia, and third-best in the East Asia & Pacific region, behind only New Zealand and Australia.

This score dropped from a perfect 100% for the first time, because more boys are now enrolling in elementary school than girls — the first time that's happened on record.

Women in parliament

The number of women in top government positions has actually gone down — from over 30% in past years to about 21% today.

25+ yrs

SOGIE Equality Bill still pending

First proposed in 2000, this bill keeps making it through House committees but has never made it into law.

2.47M

OFWs deployed in 2024

Money sent home by overseas Filipino workers makes up close to 10% of the country's entire economy each year.

0.926

Girls born per boy, 2025

Down from 0.944 in 2016 — meaning fewer girls are being born relative to boys. It's a health trend worth researching further.

Worth thinking about: when mothers work abroad, the fathers who stay home often become the main caregiver. That's a real example of "traditional" roles changing in practice — and could make a strong case study for your own analysis.

What gets repeated becomes normal

Gender roles on screen

Media doesn't just show gender roles — it repeats them, over and over, until they start to feel normal. This is where your group can add your own examples.

Filipino teleseryes, noontime shows, and ads have used the same character types for years: the mother who quietly suffers, the tough male provider, and the funny gay best friend who never gets his own storyline. Things are slowly changing — more lead characters now work, parent, and love outside those old boxes — but the older types are still around.

Here's an interesting contradiction worth digging into: the same gender-fluid identity that once had real spiritual power in the babaylan tradition is mostly just used for jokes in mainstream media today. Looking at how that role went from respected to laughed at over 500 years could be a genuinely strong angle for this section.
